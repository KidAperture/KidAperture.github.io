I think that when we remember the past, we just make ourselves more depressed. There's a big difference between looking at the past like a historian and looking at it as an individual.

When we look back at ourselves, we carry all this heavy baggage: Did I make the right choice? Was I successful? Where am I compared to where I thought I'd be?

If I took a snapshot of myself at 10 or 13, I would've seen myself as a musician with records out and on tour. Looking at myself right now, that is so far from the truth. How do we reconcile the optimal image we had of ourselves growing up with how we actually see ourselves today?

We frame nostalgia as this warm, comforting longing for the good old days, but it is never a neutral look backward. Taking our current situation and measuring it against what we thought it would be, there is this quiet grief over not measuring up to the grand blueprints our younger selves drew up.

It feels a bit cliché to say that 9/11 colored the entire growth period of our generation, but it's true—we had our infinite possibilities abruptly curtailed just as we were starting to map out our lives. Then we hit the double roadblock of perpetual wartime and a housing crash that stripped away steady employment, leaving us adrift in an economy that tried to keep us afloat with recession pop and the hollow promise of a better tomorrow.

We tried to stitch our future into a neat, cohesive plot passed down from the generations before us. But what we ended up with was a disjointed amalgamation of early-millennium optimism and deep-seated distrust, wondering how the script got flipped so completely along the way.
